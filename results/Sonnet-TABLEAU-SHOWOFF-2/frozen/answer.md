# Design Exercise 4-2 answers (instructor-requested example; numbered to match the Canvas questions)

**1. Critique of the sample design ("Income inequality varies across states").**
What works: the dumbbell (max and min county median household income per state, joined by a line) makes the within-state spread easy to see, the blue/orange legend is clear, the "x" ratio labels give a direct summary number, and the caption explains how to read it and names the extremes (WY 1.01x, VA 3.78x). States are ordered by the ratio, so the left-to-right increase carries the story.
Problems: (a) the title states a topic ("varies across states") rather than a finding. (b) The vertical axis starts at 0K and is stretched to 160K, so the bottom third of the chart is empty, and the tall, narrow format wastes space. (c) The ratio labels sit on the blue dots (3.78x collides with the VA dot), and the ratio is on the max dot even though it describes the gap. (d) The legend title "Measure Names" is Tableau jargon, and "Max."/"Min." do not say "highest/lowest county median income". (e) Only 16 selected states are shown, so a reader cannot tell if the selection is representative; the max and min are single counties, which can be tiny, so the ratio is sensitive to outliers. (f) State abbreviations appear twice (labels near dots and on the axis) and the axis header "State" is redundant. A sorted ratio bar chart or a dumbbell with a log scale, with the extreme counties named, would be cleaner.

**2. Story/Question 1 (Life Expectancy).**
Women outlive men almost everywhere, but is that female advantage changing over time and is it the same in rich and poor countries? (Answer: in high-income countries the gap peaked at 7.5 years in 1994 and has shrunk to 5.7, while in poorer groups it has grown, so the groups are converging to between 3.8 and 5.7 years.)

**3. Visualization 1 (Question 1):** `answers/figures/q1a_gender_gap_lines.png`

**4. Rationale (Question 1, primary).**
A line chart is the natural encoding for change over 64 years, and the y variable is a single calculated field (female minus male life expectancy), so the viewer reads the quantity of interest directly instead of having to compare two lines. I aggregated to the four World Bank income groups, plus a dashed gray World line as a reference, instead of 200+ countries, since that is the level where the story is visible; each group has one color from a standard categorical palette and a legend on the right. I labeled only the marks that carry the story: the high-income peak (7.5 years in 1994) and the 2023 end values, which also show the convergence. The y axis starts at zero so line heights are honest as gap sizes, with light gridlines. The title states the finding and the caption explains the calculation and the main reading. Data are descriptive: the chart does not explain why the gaps changed.

**5. Visualization 2 (Question 1):** `answers/figures/q1b_gender_gap_highlight_table.png`

**6. Rationale (Question 1, alternate).**
A highlight table (a heat map with printed numbers) puts income groups and the seven World Bank regions in rows and selected years (1960, 1970, ... 2020, 2023) in columns, with a single-hue blue scale so darker means a larger female advantage. This answers the "is it the same in different places?" part of the question that the line chart could not show without 11 overlapping lines. I split income groups from regions with a gap, sorted each block by the 2023 value, and printed every value so the table works without a tooltip. The scale starts below zero so the one place where men outlived women (South Asia in 1960 to 1970, -1.3 years) is visible as the palest cells. The title and caption point out which rows darkened (lower middle income, South Asia, Middle East & N. Africa) and which lightened (North America).

**7. Comparison (Question 1).**
The line chart is better for the trend: you can see the rise and fall of the high-income gap, the 1994 peak and the crossing/convergence of the groups, and the precise shape of the change. It is limited to a few series; adding regions would make it spaghetti, and it can only show one grouping. The highlight table is better for coverage and lookup: 11 groups, exact numbers, and a direct comparison of regions at the same year, and it surfaces the negative values in South Asia. It is worse for shape, since decade columns hide year-to-year changes (such as the 1994 high-income peak and the 2020 bump) and color differences of a year or two are hard to see; reading a trend means scanning across a row. I would put the line chart in a newspaper and the table in a report appendix.

**8. Story/Question 2 (Census).**
Did the 2020 pandemic affect unemployment differently in big-city and rural counties? (Answer: yes. Rural counties had higher unemployment every year from 2000 to 2019, but in 2020 large-metro counties jumped from 3.5% to 8.6% versus 4.1% to 7.0% for nonmetro counties, a reversal that had disappeared by 2023.)

**9. Visualization 1 (Question 2):** `answers/figures/q2a_unemployment_lines.png`

**10. Rationale (Question 2, primary).**
Unemployment is a time series, so I used a line chart over 2000 to 2023, which also gives context: the 2009 to 2010 recession spike is shown next to the 2020 spike so the reader can see that 2020 is unlike it (the ordering of rural and urban flips). Instead of nine rural-urban codes, I grouped counties into three (large metro; smaller metro; nonmetro) so that the lines are readable, using a red-orange-blue palette with the large-metro line in the warm color that the reader should follow. The rate is pooled (sum of unemployed divided by sum of labor force) rather than an average of county rates, so tiny counties do not dominate. A dotted reference line marks 2020 and two annotations give the numbers for each year compared. Counties with no 2023 code (17) are excluded, and the caption states this.

**11. Visualization 2 (Question 2):** `answers/figures/q2b_unemployment_change_bars.png`

**12. Rationale (Question 2, alternate).**
This shows the same story as a ranked comparison. A horizontal bar chart has one bar per USDA rural-urban continuum code (1 to 9), whose length is the rise in the unemployment rate from 2019 to 2020, in percentage points. Using the nine codes instead of three shows how much detail the grouped line chart hid: the effect is largest for large metros (+5.0), falls off through the smaller metros and the nonmetro codes, and is smallest for the most remote rural counties (+2.1), although not perfectly in order (codes 4 and 5, +3.3 and +3.6, exceed code 3, +3.3). Bars are colored metro (red) versus nonmetro (blue), the 2019 and 2020 rates are printed inside the bars, and a dashed reference line gives the all-county change (+4.4). Bars start at zero so lengths are comparable, and the code descriptions are written out in the axis labels.

**13. Comparison (Question 2).**
The line chart shows context: that nonmetro counties had higher unemployment for 20 years, how large the 2009 to 2010 recession was, how fast the 2020 shock faded, and that the groups converge to 3.7% by 2023. But it needs three groups, and the differences in 2020 are small relative to the axis range, so they must be read from annotations. The bar chart isolates the single 2019 to 2020 change and makes the size of the differences obvious across all nine codes, but it has no time context (a viewer cannot tell that rural counties usually fare worse), and a change score hides the level (the 2019 and 2020 values are small text). The line chart tells the "reversal" story, the bar chart the "gradient" story.

**14. Story/Question 3.**
Every kind of county has become more educated since 1990, but have rural and urban counties become more alike or further apart in the share of adults with a bachelor's degree? (Answer: the gap in percentage points grew from 13 to 20 between the largest metro and the most remote rural counties, although the relative gap shrank, since rural counties roughly doubled their share.) Census data.

**15. Visualization 3:** `answers/figures/q3_education_dumbbell.png`

**16. Rationale (Visualization 3).**
A dumbbell (connected dot) plot compares two time points, 1990 (open circle) and 2019-23 (filled circle), for each of the nine rural-urban codes, with the length of the connector showing the gain. Because the story is a change between two end points, I did not use a line chart with four time points; the intermediate years add clutter. The x axis is a common percent scale so that both the levels and the gap between rows can be compared, rows are ordered by code so the urban-to-rural gradient reads from top to bottom, and color separates metro from nonmetro counties. The printed values (start percentage, end percentage, point gain) mean no tooltips are needed. Percentages are pooled by recovering the adult population (degree-holder count divided by the reported percent), so large counties are weighted properly rather than averaging county percentages. The caption states the headline: gap 13 to 20 points, rural share about doubled.

**17. Story/Question 4.**
How much life expectancy did the pandemic take away in the hardest-hit countries, and did they get it back by 2023? (Life expectancy data.)

**18. Visualization 4:** `answers/figures/q4_covid_life_expectancy_drop.png`

**19. Rationale (Visualization 4).**
A sorted horizontal bar chart of the 15 countries with the largest fall in life expectancy at birth (both sexes) from 2019 to 2021, because the task is to rank and compare magnitudes of a change from a common zero baseline. Showing the top 15 of 217 countries is an explicit filter (the World Bank regional and income aggregates were removed first), and a dashed reference line at the median change for all countries (-0.9 years) shows how exceptional these drops are. Color encodes a calculated field, whether the 2023 value is at or above the 2019 value (blue) or not (orange, only Lebanon), which brings in the second half of the question; the gray text on the right gives the actual 2019, 2021 and 2023 values so the recovery can be checked. The title gives the headline, and the caption gives how many recovered (14 of 15). Not shown: causes, and the countries that had smaller drops. Many of the hardest-hit countries are in Latin America, which I know from outside knowledge and not from this data file (it has no region field for countries).

(2) Census: used `full_census_dataset.csv`; dropped 17 rows with no 2023 rural-urban continuum code; pooled unemployment rates as sum(Unemployed)/sum(Civilian labor force) over counties by group and year; grouped codes as 1, 2-3, 4-9; for education, recovered the number of adults 25+ per county as (degree-holder count)/(percent) and pooled by code. No outside data were added. Intermediate tables are in `work/intermediate/`, and the script is `work/make_figs.py`.
